## Context

Weir is a global engineering business supplying equipment and aftermarket services to the mining and industrial sectors. Products, customer touchpoints and internal tools had grown up independently across decades and dozens of acquisitions, leaving a fragmented digital estate that made it hard for customers — and Weir's own teams — to get a consistent view of anything.

I joined to lead product for what became the Weir Digital Ecosystem: a connected set of platforms spanning customer self-service, aftermarket sales tooling and the internal systems that support them.

## Problem

Every business unit had its own version of the same basic capability — a portal, a quoting tool, a parts catalogue — built at different times, on different stacks, to different standards. Customers experienced this as inconsistency: different logins, different data, different quality bars depending on which part of Weir they were dealing with. Internally, teams were solving the same problems repeatedly instead of building on shared foundations.

## Discovery

Rather than starting with a roadmap, I spent the first quarter mapping the existing landscape: every customer-facing system, who owned it, what it did well, and where it broke down. I ran structured conversations with regional sales and service teams, customer success managers and a sample of key accounts to understand where the friction actually was, as opposed to where it was assumed to be.

The clearest signal was that customers didn't want more features — they wanted one place to see their equipment, their orders and their service history, without having to know which internal system to ask for it.

## Product Decisions

We made a small number of deliberate bets:

- **One platform, not a rebuild.** Rather than replacing every legacy system, we built a unified experience layer that could progressively absorb capability, so teams could keep shipping without waiting for a big-bang cutover.
- **Design system first.** A shared design system was one of the earliest investments, not an afterthought, so every new surface felt like part of the same product from day one.
- **Data as the foundation.** We prioritised a consistent data model for equipment and accounts before UI polish, because inconsistent data was the root cause of most customer complaints.

## Delivery

I built and led a cross-functional product team spanning product management, design and engineering, working closely with regional stakeholders to sequence delivery around real commercial priorities rather than technical convenience. We shipped in increments, validating each stage with a small group of pilot customers before wider rollout.

## Outcomes

- Consolidated multiple regional portals into a single customer experience, live across key markets.
- Reduced the time for a customer to find equipment and service history from several minutes across multiple systems to a single search.
- Established a shared design and data foundation that has since been reused by three other internal product teams.

## Lessons Learned

Large legacy estates aren't fixed by a single big platform decision — they're fixed by making the *next* decision easier than the last one. The design system and data model mattered more to long-term velocity than any individual feature, and it took real discipline to keep investing in them while under pressure to ship visible wins.
