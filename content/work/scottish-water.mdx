## Context

Beyond the self-service programme, my time at Scottish Water covered a broader mandate: helping a large, essential-services organisation build genuine in-house product capability rather than relying solely on external delivery partners.

## Problem

Digital work happened, but it happened as projects — commissioned, delivered, and handed over — rather than as an ongoing product discipline with accountable owners and continuous iteration. That made it hard to build institutional knowledge or to improve things once they'd shipped.

## Discovery

Working across teams, I found real appetite for a different way of working, but little precedent for what it should look like inside a regulated utility. Understanding the specific constraints — regulatory reporting cycles, procurement, risk appetite — was as important as understanding user needs.

## Product Decisions

- Introduce lightweight, recurring discovery practices rather than one-off research projects.
- Establish clearer product ownership for key digital services, with accountability that outlived any single delivery project.
- Build internal capability alongside delivery, so knowledge stayed inside the organisation.

## Delivery

I worked across several concurrent initiatives, balancing hands-on delivery with the less visible work of establishing product practices that could outlast any individual programme.

## Outcomes

- Helped establish a more durable model for digital product ownership inside the organisation.
- Contributed to a measurable shift in how digital initiatives were scoped, moving from project-based to outcome-based framing.
- Left behind documentation and practices that later teams continued to build on.

## Lessons Learned

Public sector and regulated environments are frequently — and wrongly — assumed to be hostile to good product practice. The constraints are real, but so is the appetite for better ways of working, if you take the time to understand why things are the way they are before trying to change them.
