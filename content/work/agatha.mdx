## Context

Agatha started as a shared conviction with a co-founder that a specific, unglamorous problem was worth solving properly. There was no funding, no team, and no guarantee anyone else would agree it mattered. What there was: a clear point of view and a willingness to find out if it was right.

## Problem

The problem itself was narrow by design — we deliberately avoided the temptation to build something broad and impressive-sounding, and instead picked something specific enough that we could tell, quickly, whether we were right or wrong.

## Discovery

Early discovery was less "research" and more direct exposure: talking to as many potential users as possible, in person where we could, and paying close attention to what they did rather than what they said they'd do. We built the smallest possible version of the idea to get real usage signal instead of opinions.

## Product Decisions

- **Build the thinnest possible slice.** The first version deliberately did one thing, badly by feature-completeness standards but well enough to test the core assumption.
- **Optimise for learning speed, not polish.** Every week was structured around a specific question we needed answered, not a specific feature we wanted to ship.
- **Say no early and often.** Saying no to adjacent, plausible-sounding features was harder than saying yes, and mattered more.

## Delivery

As co-founder I split time across product, early design and a good deal of the unglamorous work startups actually involve — customer conversations, admin, and building things by hand that a bigger company would automate.

## Outcomes

- Validated core assumptions about the problem with real early users before committing further resource.
- Learned firsthand what breaks down in the transition from an idea to something people are asked to pay for.
- The experience directly shaped how I've approached product leadership since — with much more respect for speed, evidence and ruthless scope control.

## Lessons Learned

Founding something teaches you things no amount of product management inside an established company can. The biggest lesson was about time: as a founder, the cost of a wrong decision isn't a bad quarter, it's the company. That changes how carefully — and how fast — you learn to decide.
