I didn't set out to start a project. I just kept noticing buildings that looked temporary in a way I couldn't quite explain — a shopfront that felt already half-closed, a stairwell that felt already half-demolished — and photographing them before I could second-guess it.

It took a while before I realised the pattern was the project. Here Then Gone isn't really about architecture or decay, even though it looks like both. It's about the specific, quiet feeling of catching something in the last version of itself, without knowing exactly when that version will end.

## Why it has to be unplanned

I've tried, a few times, to plan a shoot around this idea — pick a location known for redevelopment, show up with a shot list. It never works. The photographs that hold up are the ones taken because something stopped me on an ordinary walk, not the ones taken because I'd decided in advance that a place was significant.

## What I've learned from it

Paying this kind of attention changes how you move through a city. You start noticing the small tells — a "closing down" sign that's been up for a suspiciously long time, scaffolding that's gone up around something you'd stopped seeing. Glasgow, in particular, is full of them, once you start looking.

The project doesn't have an end point. That's part of the point.
