I'd been a product manager for years before co-founding Agatha, and I assumed the core skills would transfer directly. Some did. The most important ones didn't — not because they were unrelated, but because employment quietly protects you from the full weight of your own decisions in a way founding never does.

## Speed changes when the cost is real

Inside a company, a wrong bet usually costs a bad quarter, an awkward retro, maybe a reorg. As a founder, a wrong bet can cost the company. That's not a difference in degree — it changes how you actually think, in the moment, about what's worth being certain of before you commit and what's worth deciding fast and learning from.

## Nobody is coming to rescue the roadmap

In a company, if you don't get to something this quarter, there's a decent chance someone else picks it up, or it simply waits. As a founder, if you don't get to it, it doesn't happen — there's no other team, no next sprint owned by someone else. That reframes prioritisation from "what's most important" to "what's most important that only I can do right now."

## What carried over anyway

The discovery instincts, the discomfort with vanity metrics, the habit of writing things down clearly — all of that transferred fine. What didn't transfer was the sense of safety that comes from operating inside something bigger than you. Founding removes that safety net, and you find out fast what you actually believe once it's gone.
