Ask most early-stage teams what their strategy is, and you'll usually get a reasonable answer. Ask what they did last week, and you'll often find a list of things that have almost nothing to do with it. The gap isn't a lack of strategic thinking — it's a lack of strategic discipline.

This shows up as opportunism dressed up as ambition: the inbound partnership that's "too good to pass up," the feature request from a loud customer who isn't actually the target customer, the market that looks adjacent enough to chase. Individually, each decision seems sensible. Collectively, they add up to a company doing five different things moderately well instead of one thing very well.

## The real skill

The skill that actually separates focused companies from unfocused ones isn't coming up with a strategy — most founders can do that in an afternoon. It's saying no to good opportunities that don't fit it, repeatedly, under pressure, when saying yes would feel like momentum.

## A useful test

Before saying yes to anything that isn't clearly inside the current strategy, it's worth asking: if this works, does it make the core thing stronger, or does it just make the company busier? Busy and strong are not the same thing, and it's easy to mistake one for the other when you're moving fast.
