Most teams treat discovery as a gate: a phase you pass through on the way to "real" work. You do some interviews, write a brief, and then get on with building. The problem is that framing turns discovery into theatre — something you perform to justify a decision you'd already made.

The teams that actually benefit from discovery treat it differently. They treat it as a habit that continues for the life of the product, not a milestone that precedes it. The question isn't "have we done discovery" — it's "when did we last learn something that changed our mind?" If the honest answer is "a while ago," that's the problem, regardless of how much research happened at the start.

## The tell

The clearest sign a team is doing discovery as theatre rather than practice: nobody can point to a decision discovery actually changed. If every research readout confirms the roadmap that already existed, either you were remarkably right from the start, or the research wasn't really allowed to disagree with you.

## What good looks like

Good discovery is uncomfortable. It surfaces things you didn't want to be true — that the problem you're excited about isn't the one users care most about, that your elegant solution doesn't fit how people actually work, that the metric you picked doesn't mean what you thought it meant.

If discovery never makes you uncomfortable, you're probably not doing it — you're confirming it.
